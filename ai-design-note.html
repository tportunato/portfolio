01

Where I start

I only let a model do work where I'd spot the mistake myself.

I built these tools in 2026 while working as an investment analyst on a pan-European logistics fund. Each one started with a task I was doing by hand and didn't enjoy: chasing missing numbers before an LP report, re-keying valuation reports into a tracker, pulling the same Geneva planning layers one by one.

My rule for where AI goes is simple. If a step needs judgement, the analyst keeps it. If a step is mostly copying, reformatting or translating, a model can take the first pass, provided the result is quick to check against the source. In practice that means extraction and first drafts, and never the valuation call or the investment conclusion.

The risk I worry about most is people using these models for work they couldn't do without them. The output reads well, so errors last longer than they should. Knowing what a sensible cap rate or a stretched covenant looks like comes from years of doing the work by hand, and I'd rather my tools didn't skip that step.

The other constraint is confidentiality. Fund data, tenant credit files and loan terms shouldn't go into a model unless the firm knows where that data is stored, whether it's retained, and who can see it. That's why every demo here runs on illustrative or public data only.

How the demos work

The demos don't call a live model. Where a tool shows an AI briefing, memo or answer, it's a pre-written example of what a live call would return. That keeps API keys out of the browser and avoids paying for every visit. Parcelle has no AI layer and queries Geneva's public data services live. All portfolio and deal figures are illustrative.

02

LP Report Generator

Most of the time on a quarterly LP report goes on getting the data right, not on the writing. Missing figures turn into vague sentences, and unchecked figures turn into errors that an LP will notice before you do.

So the tool puts data first. You fill in the asset table, the debt schedule and the NAV bridge, and a set of rules flags what I'd want to catch before drafting: WALB under three years, LTV close to its covenant, occupancy below 90%, unhedged debt maturing within 18 months, a valuation uplift on a half-empty building. Critical flags block drafting. Warnings stay on screen until someone deals with them.

Drafted text is marked so you can see what was generated and what was written or edited by hand. Each section is signed off separately, editing a signed section reopens it, and every change is logged. That matters when compliance reads the report before it goes out.

03

Asset Manager

On a multi-country fund the inputs come from everywhere: valuers, property managers, lenders, credit agencies, each in its own format and on its own timetable. Pulling them into one view takes hours and very little thought.

Asset Manager puts the portfolio on one screen: GAV, rent, debt, LTV and WALT, how each has moved since acquisition, and a drill-down per asset with quarterly history, tenancy and financing. Quarterly updates carry forward anything that hasn't changed. Alert rules watch for LTV and ICR getting close to covenant, short WALT, upcoming loan maturities, falling valuations and tenant credit downgrades.

The AI features sit on top: an asset or portfolio briefing, free questions across the portfolio, and lease questions answered with a pointer to the clause, including for French, Italian and Dutch leases. In the demo these are scripted examples. Import from the CSV template works as is; free-format PDF and spreadsheet import needs the live model.

04

Parcelle

Geneva's planning data is public, but it's split across separate SITG layers: cadastre, zoning, building footprints, permits, PLQs, raising-the-roof potential and the OCEN heating index. Building a view of one parcel means opening each of them in turn.

Parcelle queries those layers live from the canton's services and shows them together for each parcel, with a link to the official RDPPF extract and to any PLQ document. The scan tool screens a commune by zone, plot size and how much of the permitted volume is already built, and exports the shortlist to CSV.

Every figure shows where it came from. Where something is inferred, such as commune rent levels or a price index modelled from tax data, it's labelled as such, and the data tab lists which public sources are connected and which aren't yet. Owner identity is left out on purpose. There's no AI here: it's a screening tool, and deciding what a parcel is worth stays with the analyst.

05

IC Lens

Excel models, memos and decks are built for depth on one deal. Investment committees usually compare several at once, and that comparison tends to happen in someone's head.

IC Lens puts three to five buyout deals side by side on entry valuation, leverage, returns, growth assumptions and fit with the mandate, with a heatmap against thresholds so outliers stand out. It's a visual layer. It doesn't check whether the inputs are right, and it doesn't replace the work behind them.

Two AI features are mocked up in the demo: a comparative note across the deals, and extraction of deal figures from a teaser or CIM with low-confidence fields flagged for checking.

06

Radar

When a deal is moving, you need a market view in minutes, and the comparables are usually sitting in a spreadsheet nobody wants to open.

Radar maps around ninety illustrative logistics assets across the main Dutch corridors: Venlo, Tilburg and Waalwijk, Rotterdam, Schiphol, Almere, Roosendaal. You can filter by size, owner type, hold period, clear height and vacancy, or use preset screens for likely off-market sellers, value-add, core income and grade A stock.

Asset commentary and data upload are scripted in the demo. The obvious next step is connecting it to Kadaster transaction records, so new comparables show up when they're registered rather than when a broker mentions them.
